Architecture Overview

Level Of Tessellation Boolean Collision Body Projection

How a simulated fluid finds the real walls — and the real openings — of an arbitrary imported 3D model. Not a hand-tuned primitive, not a bounding box, but the actual triangle mesh of whatever tank, pipe, manifold, or chamber was dropped into the scene: projected into a signed-distance collision body that adapts its resolution to the geometry, then Boolean-tested part against part so fluid knows which cavities connect. Cheap enough to bake in seconds, sampled every frame on a GPU, precise enough not to leak through a thin wall or a tight joint.

01

The Problem// why not just a primitive

Arc Flow (the web particle-fluid simulator at /arc-flow.html) and ArcLake-iOS (the native SceneKit/Metal app) both let a person import a real 3D asset — a fuel tank, a propulsion manifold, a length of plumbing with joints — and then run a fluid or particle simulation that has to flow correctly through that exact geometry. Not a sphere standing in for the tank. The actual mesh, however many triangles it has, however it was modeled, including the case where it wasn't modeled as a sealed solid at all.

That last point is the hard part. These parts are typically thin, single-surface shells exported from CAD or glTF — a tank wall is one layer of triangles, not an inner and outer skin — and they are deliberately non-watertight: a tank has a neck, a pipe has two open ends, a combustion chamber has an entrance and a nozzle throat. Those openings are exactly where the fluid is supposed to flow through. Any collision technique that assumes a closed, watertight solid breaks on contact with this geometry.

Core requirement

Given an arbitrary imported mesh — open-ended, thin-shelled, hundreds of thousands of triangles — produce, once per Simulate press, a fast "am I inside or outside, and how far to the nearest wall" query that a GPU shader can call millions of times per frame.

The answer both platforms converge on is a baked signed distance field (SDF): a grid of "distance to nearest surface, negative inside" samples, built once from the mesh and then just looked up — interpolated — during simulation. The rest of this page is about how that grid gets built correctly and fast, and how it doubles as more than collision.

02

Level of Tessellation// where resolution is spent

A single uniform grid over an entire imported assembly forces an impossible choice: resolution fine enough to resolve a tight joint or a narrow feed line means an enormous grid everywhere else, including the wide-open interior of a tank where nothing interesting is happening. Level of tessellation — sampling resolution that adapts to where detail actually matters — is the way out. Arc Flow builds three kinds of grid and packs them into one atlas.

Coarse grid

One grid over the whole scene. The budget is 20³ voxels, spread per axis by the scene's aspect ratio (a tall stack gets more voxels along Y).

Bulk-interior accuracy only: the open middle of a tank, a long straight run, the exterior far from any wall.

Per-part grids

One adaptive grid for each classified part (chambers first, then containers, then pipes; up to 24 of them), sized from the part's bounding box.

The part's thinnest dimension gets at least 14 voxels where its budget allows (e.g. 4000 voxels for a chamber, 1200 for a pipe), never more than 48 per axis — independent of how many triangles the import happens to have.

Joint grids

A small 14³ grid in a padded box around every place two different parts meet (or several openings converge on one chamber). The box is a fraction of the scene, so this is real resolution exactly at the seam. All grids together are capped at 48 fine regions.

Fine regions are found by position

Which grid answers a query is decided by where the query point is, not by where it is supposed to be. In the particle shader, resolveFineIdx tries the cheap hint first (the region the particle's current path segment belongs to) and then tests the point against every fine region's box. This matters because a particle's rendered position includes a lateral offset from its path: for part of any segment leading into a joint, a path-based hint would point at the wrong region and let a particle already pushed into wall territory fall back to the coarse grid — far too coarse for a narrow pipe beside a tank wall, and the cause of the “seeping through the cracks” failure. Joint regions are tested before per-part regions, per-part regions before the coarse grid.

03

Why Brute Force Doesn't Scale// the spatial hash

Building either grid means, for every voxel, finding the nearest point on the mesh. The bundled Arc Flow propulsion demo alone totals roughly 223,000 triangles across seven parts. Checking a voxel against every triangle is O(triangles) per sample; a coarse grid plus a dozen-plus fine joint grids is thousands of voxels — O(voxels × triangles) in total, which lands in the tens of billions of triangle-distance tests for a scene this size. That doesn't just run slow — it makes the whole technique unusable.

The fix: bucket triangles into a spatial hash

Before sampling any voxel, every triangle is dropped into a coarse 3D grid of cells, keyed by which cells its own bounding box touches (not just its centroid — a large triangle spanning several cells must be visible to a query landing in any of them). A nearest-triangle query then starts at the query point's own cell and expands outward ring by ring, stopping once it has a confident answer — searching a handful of nearby triangles instead of the whole mesh. The per-voxel cost scales with local triangle density, not the model's total triangle count.

Honest case study — the ~150s freeze

This acceleration structure was already in place and still froze the Simulate button for roughly 144–160 seconds on the bundled demo, confirmed by profiling. The root cause: the spatial hash capped itself at 4,096 cells regardless of triangle count, so once a mesh passed about 24,576 triangles, cell density stopped scaling — a 223k-triangle scene ended up with ~54 triangles per cell instead of the intended ~6, and every ring-search had to test ~9× more candidates than the structure was designed for, on every single voxel. Raising the cell-count cap so it keeps scaling with triangle count (targetCells = clamp(triCount / 6, 8, 65536)) restored the intended density and, combined with lowering the coarse grid from 28³ to 20³ voxels and caching each part's world-space triangles instead of re-walking them for every fine region, turned a 2–3 minute hang into a build that yields a frame between steps and finishes in seconds.

This is the real engineering history of the system, not a sanitized version of it — the bug, its exact numeric signature, and the fix are all part of what makes LOTBCBP fast enough to use today.

04

Inside or Outside: The Pseudo-Normal Sign Test// why ray-parity fails here

Finding the nearest triangle only gives an unsigned distance. Signing it — deciding whether a point is inside (fluid-legal) or outside (collision) — is where open, non-watertight shells break the textbook approach.

The textbook approach, and why it fails

The standard technique for a closed mesh is ray-parity: cast a ray from the query point in a fixed direction and count surface crossings — odd means inside. That assumes the mesh seals completely. A tank with an open neck, or a pipe with two open ends, doesn't: a ray cast from deep inside such a shell can exit cleanly through one of those openings without crossing anything else, under-counting crossings and reporting a point in the dead center of the part's interior as outside. This was confirmed directly — a probe at the center of an open-ended test cylinder's interior read as a false positive under ray-parity.

The fix: sign from the nearest surface's own normal

Rather than casting a ray, LOTBCBP uses the pseudo-normal technique (Baerentzen & Aanaes): once the globally closest triangle to a point is found, the sign is just 

sign(dot(point − closestPoint, faceNormalOfClosestTriangle))

— negative (inside, fluid-legal) when the point sits on the side the triangle's outward normal does not point toward. This only ever looks at the nearest surface, so it has no blind spot for open ends; it degrades gracefully only right at an opening's edge, which is precisely the region where fluid is allowed to cross anyway. When a point's closest feature is a shared edge or vertex between adjacent triangles (common right at a joint or crease), the normals of every tied triangle are angle-accumulated into one pseudo-normal rather than picking whichever triangle happened to be visited first — so the sign doesn't depend on triangle visit order.

One more correction this requires: a mirrored part (negative-scale world matrix, common for a mirrored half of an assembly) flips triangle winding, which flips every face normal on that part — and with it, every sign computed near it. Triangle vertex order is reversed for any part whose world matrix has a negative determinant, restoring outward-pointing winding before the sign test ever runs.

05

Collision Body Projection// mesh → signed-distance body

Projection is the step that turns whatever mesh was imported into a collision body the GPU can query, without ever editing the visible model. It is built from the pieces above plus four more, all of which exist in the Arc Flow app today:

  • Spatial hash, then the nearest surface. Every part's triangles are bucketed into a 3D hash keyed by bounding box, so a voxel only tests nearby triangles (section 03).
  • Pseudo-normal sign. The sign of each sample comes from the nearest triangle's own normal rather than ray parity, so a thin, open-ended, non-watertight shell still has a well-defined inside: the hollow side of its single surface (section 04).
  • Per-part adaptive grids and position-resolved fine regions. Each part gets a grid sized from its bounding box and thinnest dimension, and queries pick their grid by position (section 02).
  • Virtual triangle subdivision. A sparsely modeled import — a tank that is forty big triangles — still needs an accurate body. Any triangle much longer than the grid's voxel (about 1.8 voxels) is bisected along its longest edge for the SDF build only. Children keep the parent's winding, so the sign test is unchanged. Where the two ends of a split edge carry smooth vertex normals, the new midpoint is Phong-projected toward the curved surface the coarse polygon stands for; flat-shaded faces stay exactly flat, so plates and boxes are not rounded. The count of added triangles is capped, and the visible mesh and the cached world triangles are never modified.
  • Tube-fitted analytic bore. A long narrow feed line would need an enormous box grid to put even six voxels across its bore. Instead, thin or elongated parts (pipe-classified, or with a bounding-box aspect of 6 or more) get their centerline and inner bore radius fitted from the mesh vertices and are represented as a chain of analytic capsules: exact across the bore, at no grid cost. The tube is consulted first by the particle shader, the ray-march occlusion and the live-leak CPU field; mesh grids stay in charge for tanks, joints and wall exteriors. The same fit supplies the bore area along the pipe for the channel flow physics.

Parts that are closed solids with a real wall thickness (a shelled body with inner and outer surfaces) are analysed so that the cavity is the space inside the inner layer and the solid is the outer layer, which is what lets one projection serve both fluid containment and solid-body overlap tests. After the bake, an LTCBP live watch probes the baked field next to the exact surface during simulation and raises or lowers resolution (and rebuilds on deformation or part movement) when the error measured in voxels is too high or persistently tiny.

What projection guarantees

One consistent query — “signed distance to the real wall, negative on the fluid-legal side” — for a particle, a ray-marcher and a live leak, built from the exact imported triangles, at a resolution that follows the geometry rather than the tessellation of the file.

06

Boolean Projection// how cavities meet

A collision body says where the walls are. It does not say which parts are connected. Real assemblies are exported as separate, touching parts: a container, a feed pipe, a combustion chamber with a nozzle. Fluid should flow container → pipe → chamber → nozzle only where the hollow of one part actually opens into the hollow of the next. Boolean projection decides that by intersecting the parts' cavities.

The cavity-intersection test, per part pair

  • Gate. The two parts' bounding boxes must overlap by more than a fraction of the smaller part's thinnest extent, so a sliver or a shared flush face is not treated as an intersection.
  • Label. Sample a small grid (9 per axis) across the overlap box. Each point is tested against each part's own mesh with the signed distance above: inside A only, inside B only, inside both, or inside neither. A thin cavity can slip between samples, so near-misses are refined locally, three levels of 3³ samples at one third the pitch each, before a pair is called a real gap.
  • Through-cavity continuity. Flood-fill the labelled samples. A through-joint is one connected region that contains exclusive samples of both parts: the hollow path really crosses from A's own interior into B's. Points inside both alone do not count, so a part sitting wholly inside another is not mistaken for a joint. Closed volumes pushed through each other, with a substantial shared volume, are treated as an intersection joint as well.
  • Locate the opening. The joint's centre is the mean of the shared samples touching an exclusive neighbour, i.e. the wall-crossing zone, and its radius comes from that zone's cross-section.

The implied wall cut-out

Where two cavities intersect, the result is, in effect, a Boolean subtract of the shared cavity from the two parts' walls: the exit opening of one part lines up with the entrance of the next, and the wall that would otherwise stand between them is treated as open. The app does not rewrite the meshes to do this. The cut-out is implied: the joint is recorded as a synthetic exit/entrance pair at the overlap, facing along the upstream-to-downstream axis (containers rank upstream of pipes, pipes of chambers), and the flow graph, the fine joint region and the link diagnostics all read it. Fluid then flows through the opening as though the wall had been cut. A manually marked exit and entrance remain the source of truth: pairs already joined by a real link are skipped, and a marked pair counts as sealed when its two openings are within a tolerance proportional to their radii, or when the user has confirmed the seal.

Connected

Cavities intersect, or the openings meet. The joint is a seam of the flow graph and gets a fine collision region around it.

Separated or pressed shut

Separated (a real gap): the fluid spills into the surroundings at the open end. Pressed against a surface whose cavity does not intersect (a pipe end touching a plain wall): the opening is plugged, so there is no flow and, while the parts are only briefly displaced, no spill. The same plug test applies to a nozzle that is blocked.

The result is recomputed only for a pair whose parts moved relative to each other; results are cached in the local frame of the assembly, so moving or rotating a whole clone only re-projects cached joints.

Reusable for Blender

The cavity labelling, flood-fill continuity and wall cut-out logic does not depend on fluid at all — it is a statement about two hollow meshes. That is why a Boolean option in the Arc Flow Blender extension can use the same cavity logic: intersect the cavities of two parts and open the wall between them.

07

Packing a 3D Grid Into a 2D Texture// the tiling trick

GPUs sample 2D textures natively in every WebGL1/WebGL2 and mobile Metal context without relying on optional 3D-texture extensions. Rather than requiring one, each SDF grid is packed as a tiled 2D-as-3D texture: every Z-slice of the res³ voxel grid is laid out as its own square tile, tiles arranged left-to-right/top-to-bottom across one square image.

Each Z-slice of the voxel grid becomes one square tile in a single 2D image. A shader recovers (x,y,z) → (tileX, tileY) by tileCol = z mod tilesPerRow, tileRow = floor(z / tilesPerRow) — one ordinary sampler2D, no 3D-texture support required.

One shared atlas for every joint

Every fine grid shares the same resolution, so instead of one texture per joint — which would need a dynamically-indexed sampler array, poorly supported on mobile GL and compile-time-sized in GLSL regardless — all fine grids are packed into one shared atlas texture: each joint gets its own fixed-size block of Z-tiles within that single image, addressed by a per-particle "which fine region, if any" index uniform. The whole fine-collision system costs exactly one extra texture sampler, never N of them.

Manual trilinear, on purpose

The packed textures are sampled with NearestFilter, not linear filtering — and the shader does its own trilinear interpolation across the eight surrounding voxels by hand. Two reasons: hardware bilinear filtering only blends within one Z-tile, never across a tile seam, so letting the GPU interpolate near a tile boundary would blend two unrelated regions of the packed image together instead of the two neighboring Z-slices the SDF actually needs. And a FloatType texture with hardware LinearFilter silently becomes an incomplete texture on a mobile GPU lacking the OES_texture_float_linear extension — sampling as all-zero, which would read as "distance 0 everywhere" and disable collision outright.

08

The Bake Pipeline// end to end, once per Simulate press

01
Collect world-space triangles per part (cached — walked once, reused everywhere)
02
Boolean projection: test part pairs, record joints and the flow graph; diagnose each link
03
Build the spatial hash; virtually subdivide long triangles for the build only
04
Sample the coarse grid (20³ budget); sign via pseudo-normal
05
Sample per-part adaptive grids and per-joint grids (14³); fit analytic bores for thin parts
06
Pack all as tiled 2D textures in one atlas, upload, hand to the shaders

Each step yields a frame back to the browser and updates a loading overlay — this is what turned an opaque multi-minute freeze into a build that always shows visible progress, however long the real work takes on a given device. The link diagnostics run time-sliced for the same reason.

09

One Field, Two Jobs// collision doubles as density

The architectural principle behind LOTBCBP, stated plainly: the same baked field that tells a particle "don't cross this wall" is the same field that tells a renderer "how much stuff is here to draw." Collision data and the density field for ray-marched volumetric rendering are not two systems that happen to agree — they're one dataset read two ways.

Web — Arc Flow

The coarse + fine SDF atlas built above feeds the particle vertex shader directly: every particle samples sdfAt(p) each frame to resolve collision against the real mesh, preferring its nearest fine joint region when inside one and falling back to the coarse whole-scene grid otherwise.

Native — ArcLake-iOS

The conceptual counterpart is ArcSparseVoxelGrid — a solid/cavity collision grid built by rasterizing triangles against voxel AABBs (an Akenine-Möller separating-axis test), with the same coarse-main / fine-subdivision split (fineSolidCells) at tight passages. ArcVolumetricFluidRenderer then ray-marches a density volume that reads this same voxel grid's solid mask directly as its hard density-zero / occlusion term, alongside the simulated fluid's own per-cell density — one baked collision/density volume driving both the physics and what gets drawn.

This is the stated direction for where the web side is headed too: use the same collision data that already keeps particles out of walls as the density field a ray marcher reads, rather than building and maintaining a second geometry-aware structure just for rendering. One bake, two consumers — simulate against it, and render by marching through it.

10

Same Math, Second Job: Quad RetopologyBlender ext. v0.2

The Arc Flow Blender extension already has to compute a collision-body projection and a Boolean projection. Both are geometry operations on triangle meshes: nearest-surface queries, signed inside/outside tests, cavity intersections. That is most of the math that topology work needs, so the extension reuses it for a second job: an extra button that acts as a Quad Remesh / Retopologize tool for characters and mechanical 3D assets. It ships with the v0.2 release of the extension.

What the tool does

  • Scan and record. It walks the faces and identifies the triangles, and the faces with an odd number of edges (n-gons with 5, 7, … edges), which cannot be split evenly into quads. They are recorded as the work list.
  • Triangles to balanced, symmetric quads. Triangles are converted to quads in a balanced, symmetric way, so that the result does not skew the way a naive pair-and-merge does.
  • Measure the small/large face threshold per region. Rather than one global density, it measures the boundary between small and large faces region by region. On a character, the crease along a lip is such a region: it should receive an appropriate density of small quads along the crease, while the surrounding surface keeps a coarser, appropriate level of tessellation. Remaining triangles are converted to quad equivalents.
  • Balance and mirror. The result is balanced across the whole mesh, and mirror symmetry is enforced so that left and right halves match.

The goal is a quick topology fix and optimization for sculpts and CAD imports, so they work well with Arc Flow or anything else in Blender, without a manual retopology pass first.

Schematic of the intent, not a measured result. Quad size follows the region: fine where the crease needs it, coarser on the surrounding surface, with the two halves mirrored.
Why it belongs here

A collision body is only as good as the mesh it is projected from, and the tessellation of CAD exports and raw sculpts is often the worst case for it: long thin triangles, uneven density, odd faces. Cleaning topology with the same projection math closes the loop — a model prepared in Blender comes into Arc Flow with a mesh that is both easier to project and easier to edit.
