What is a British Thermal Unit?

The British thermal unit (Btu) is a unit of heat energy that traces back to early steam-engine era engineering in the United States and Great Britain. It was originally defined around a simple, physical experiment: the quantity of heat needed to raise one pound of liquid water by one degree Fahrenheit, measured at roughly normal sea-level atmospheric pressure (about 14.7 psi).

Because the heat capacity of water shifts slightly with starting temperature, several near-identical Btu definitions exist (Thermochemical, 59°F, 60°F, 39°F, and the "IT" / International Table definition). This calculator uses the IT definition (≈1055.06 J), the value most often used in modern engineering tables.

For intuition: one Btu is roughly the heat released by burning a single wooden kitchen match, or the energy needed to lift a one-pound weight about 778 feet straight up. In HVAC work, the rate Btu/h is converted to watts — the SI unit of power — and large cooling systems are often rated in tons of refrigeration (12,000 Btu/h).
