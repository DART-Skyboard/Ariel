Architecture Overview

Level of Tessellation Collision Body

How a simulated fluid finds the real walls of an arbitrary imported 3D model — not a hand-tuned primitive, not a bounding box, but the actual triangle mesh of whatever tank, pipe, manifold, or chamber was dropped into the scene — cheaply enough to bake in seconds and sample every frame on a GPU, and precisely enough to not leak through a thin wall or a tight joint.

01

The Problem// why not just a primitive

Arc Flow (the web particle-fluid simulator at /arc-flow.html) and ArcLake-iOS (the native SceneKit/Metal app) both let a person import a real 3D asset — a fuel tank, a propulsion manifold, a length of plumbing with joints — and then run a fluid or particle simulation that has to flow correctly through that exact geometry. Not a sphere standing in for the tank. The actual mesh, however many triangles it has, however it was modeled, including the case where it wasn't modeled as a sealed solid at all.

That last point is the hard part. These parts are typically thin, single-surface shells exported from CAD or glTF — a tank wall is one layer of triangles, not an inner and outer skin — and they are deliberately non-watertight: a tank has a neck, a pipe has two open ends, a combustion chamber has an entrance and a nozzle throat. Those openings are exactly where the fluid is supposed to flow through. Any collision technique that assumes a closed, watertight solid breaks on contact with this geometry.

Core requirement

Given an arbitrary imported mesh — open-ended, thin-shelled, hundreds of thousands of triangles — produce, once per Simulate press, a fast "am I inside or outside, and how far to the nearest wall" query that a GPU shader can call millions of times per frame.

The answer both platforms converge on is a baked signed distance field (SDF): a grid of "distance to nearest surface, negative inside" samples, built once from the mesh and then just looked up — interpolated — during simulation. The rest of this page is about how that grid gets built correctly and fast, and how it doubles as more than collision.

02

Two Tiers: Coarse Bulk, Fine Joints// LOTCB's namesake idea

A single uniform grid over an entire imported assembly forces an impossible choice: resolution fine enough to resolve a tight joint where two parts' cross-sections meet means an enormous grid everywhere else, including the wide-open interior of a tank where nothing interesting is happening at all. Level of tessellation — sampling resolution that adapts to where detail actually matters — is the way out.

Coarse grid

One grid over the whole scene, low resolution (Arc Flow: 20³ voxels).

Covers bulk interior — the open middle of a tank, a long straight pipe run — where "am I inside" barely changes voxel to voxel. Doesn't need to be fine; was never meant to carry fine detail.

Fine grids

One small, high-resolution grid per joint (Arc Flow: 14³ voxels, but over a box a fraction of the scene's size — real resolution there).

Covers exactly the regions where two parts' real surfaces come close together, or where several openings converge — where a coarse sample would miss a wall entirely.

What counts as a "joint"?

A joint region is identified wherever the flow graph says two different parts' real cross-sections meet (an exit opening on one part lining up with an entrance on the next), padded out by a multiple of the opening's own radius so the fine box comfortably contains the actual seam. A chamber's own openings — its entrances and its nozzle throat — get the same treatment even without crossing a part boundary, since several feed lines converging into one part is its own tight, high-triangle-density region. The region count is capped so an elaborate import can't blow out the build budget.

At query time, a shader checks whether the point it's asking about falls inside one of these padded fine boxes; if so, it reads the fine grid for precision, and otherwise falls back to the coarse grid for everywhere else.

03

Why Brute Force Doesn't Scale// the spatial hash

Building either grid means, for every voxel, finding the nearest point on the mesh. The bundled Arc Flow propulsion demo alone totals roughly 223,000 triangles across seven parts. Checking a voxel against every triangle is O(triangles) per sample; a coarse grid plus a dozen-plus fine joint grids is thousands of voxels — O(voxels × triangles) in total, which lands in the tens of billions of triangle-distance tests for a scene this size. That doesn't just run slow — it makes the whole technique unusable.

The fix: bucket triangles into a spatial hash

Before sampling any voxel, every triangle is dropped into a coarse 3D grid of cells, keyed by which cells its own bounding box touches (not just its centroid — a large triangle spanning several cells must be visible to a query landing in any of them). A nearest-triangle query then starts at the query point's own cell and expands outward ring by ring, stopping once it has a confident answer — searching a handful of nearby triangles instead of the whole mesh. The per-voxel cost scales with local triangle density, not the model's total triangle count.

Honest case study — the ~150s freeze

This acceleration structure was already in place and still froze the Simulate button for roughly 144–160 seconds on the bundled demo, confirmed by profiling. The root cause: the spatial hash capped itself at 4,096 cells regardless of triangle count, so once a mesh passed about 24,576 triangles, cell density stopped scaling — a 223k-triangle scene ended up with ~54 triangles per cell instead of the intended ~6, and every ring-search had to test ~9× more candidates than the structure was designed for, on every single voxel. Raising the cell-count cap so it keeps scaling with triangle count (targetCells = clamp(triCount / 6, 8, 65536)) restored the intended density and, combined with lowering the coarse grid from 28³ to 20³ voxels and caching each part's world-space triangles instead of re-walking them for every fine region, turned a 2–3 minute hang into a build that yields a frame between steps and finishes in seconds.

This is the real engineering history of the system, not a sanitized version of it — the bug, its exact numeric signature, and the fix are all part of what makes LOTCB fast enough to use today.

04

Inside or Outside: The Pseudo-Normal Sign Test// why ray-parity fails here

Finding the nearest triangle only gives an unsigned distance. Signing it — deciding whether a point is inside (fluid-legal) or outside (collision) — is where open, non-watertight shells break the textbook approach.

The textbook approach, and why it fails

The standard technique for a closed mesh is ray-parity: cast a ray from the query point in a fixed direction and count surface crossings — odd means inside. That assumes the mesh seals completely. A tank with an open neck, or a pipe with two open ends, doesn't: a ray cast from deep inside such a shell can exit cleanly through one of those openings without crossing anything else, under-counting crossings and reporting a point in the dead center of the part's interior as outside. This was confirmed directly — a probe at the center of an open-ended test cylinder's interior read as a false positive under ray-parity.

The fix: sign from the nearest surface's own normal

Rather than casting a ray, LOTCB uses the pseudo-normal technique (Baerentzen & Aanaes): once the globally closest triangle to a point is found, the sign is just 

sign(dot(point − closestPoint, faceNormalOfClosestTriangle))

— negative (inside, fluid-legal) when the point sits on the side the triangle's outward normal does not point toward. This only ever looks at the nearest surface, so it has no blind spot for open ends; it degrades gracefully only right at an opening's edge, which is precisely the region where fluid is allowed to cross anyway. When a point's closest feature is a shared edge or vertex between adjacent triangles (common right at a joint or crease), the normals of every tied triangle are angle-accumulated into one pseudo-normal rather than picking whichever triangle happened to be visited first — so the sign doesn't depend on triangle visit order.

One more correction this requires: a mirrored part (negative-scale world matrix, common for a mirrored half of an assembly) flips triangle winding, which flips every face normal on that part — and with it, every sign computed near it. Triangle vertex order is reversed for any part whose world matrix has a negative determinant, restoring outward-pointing winding before the sign test ever runs.

05

Packing a 3D Grid Into a 2D Texture// the tiling trick

GPUs sample 2D textures natively in every WebGL1/WebGL2 and mobile Metal context without relying on optional 3D-texture extensions. Rather than requiring one, each SDF grid is packed as a tiled 2D-as-3D texture: every Z-slice of the res³ voxel grid is laid out as its own square tile, tiles arranged left-to-right/top-to-bottom across one square image.

Each Z-slice of the voxel grid becomes one square tile in a single 2D image. A shader recovers (x,y,z) → (tileX, tileY) by tileCol = z mod tilesPerRow, tileRow = floor(z / tilesPerRow) — one ordinary sampler2D, no 3D-texture support required.

One shared atlas for every joint

Every fine grid shares the same resolution, so instead of one texture per joint — which would need a dynamically-indexed sampler array, poorly supported on mobile GL and compile-time-sized in GLSL regardless — all fine grids are packed into one shared atlas texture: each joint gets its own fixed-size block of Z-tiles within that single image, addressed by a per-particle "which fine region, if any" index uniform. The whole fine-collision system costs exactly one extra texture sampler, never N of them.

Manual trilinear, on purpose

The packed textures are sampled with NearestFilter, not linear filtering — and the shader does its own trilinear interpolation across the eight surrounding voxels by hand. Two reasons: hardware bilinear filtering only blends within one Z-tile, never across a tile seam, so letting the GPU interpolate near a tile boundary would blend two unrelated regions of the packed image together instead of the two neighboring Z-slices the SDF actually needs. And a FloatType texture with hardware LinearFilter silently becomes an incomplete texture on a mobile GPU lacking the OES_texture_float_linear extension — sampling as all-zero, which would read as "distance 0 everywhere" and disable collision outright.

06

The Bake Pipeline// end to end, once per Simulate press

01
Collect world-space triangles per part (cached — walked once, reused by every region that touches that part)
02
Build spatial hash over the whole scene's triangles
03
Sample coarse grid (20³) — sign via pseudo-normal
04
Identify joint regions from the flow graph + chamber openings
05
Per joint: filter to nearby triangles, sample fine grid (14³), write into shared atlas block
06
Pack both as tiled 2D textures, upload, hand to the particle shader

Each step yields a frame back to the browser and updates a loading overlay — this is what turned an opaque multi-minute freeze into a build that always shows visible progress, however long the real work takes on a given device.

07

One Field, Two Jobs// collision doubles as density

The architectural principle behind LOTCB, stated plainly: the same baked field that tells a particle "don't cross this wall" is the same field that tells a renderer "how much stuff is here to draw." Collision data and the density field for ray-marched volumetric rendering are not two systems that happen to agree — they're one dataset read two ways.

Web — Arc Flow

The coarse + fine SDF atlas built above feeds the particle vertex shader directly: every particle samples sdfAt(p) each frame to resolve collision against the real mesh, preferring its nearest fine joint region when inside one and falling back to the coarse whole-scene grid otherwise.

Native — ArcLake-iOS

The conceptual counterpart is ArcSparseVoxelGrid — a solid/cavity collision grid built by rasterizing triangles against voxel AABBs (an Akenine-Möller separating-axis test), with the same coarse-main / fine-subdivision split (fineSolidCells) at tight passages. ArcVolumetricFluidRenderer then ray-marches a density volume that reads this same voxel grid's solid mask directly as its hard density-zero / occlusion term, alongside the simulated fluid's own per-cell density — one baked collision/density volume driving both the physics and what gets drawn.

This is the stated direction for where the web side is headed too: use the same collision data that already keeps particles out of walls as the density field a ray marcher reads, rather than building and maintaining a second geometry-aware structure just for rendering. One bake, two consumers — simulate against it, and render by marching through it.
